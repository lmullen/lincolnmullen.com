_The Pudding_ is one of my favorite sites on the internet, full of fascinating and whimsical data visualizations. Occasionally the site takes on historical topics, as they recently did with historical menus from the New York Public Library. The site offered two looks at the menus. The first is an [exploratory interface](https://pudding.cool/2026/06/menu-collection/) to find the menus in the NYPL collection by city or other details, and to look at them directly. The second is a [narrative graphic](https://pudding.cool/2026/06/menu-story/) explaining what can be learned from the menus.

{{< image src="pudding-menu-visualizations.png" alt="A screenshot of the thumbnails of the two visualizations from The Pudding website" >}}

The two types of approach---explanatory and exploratory---offer a helpful comparison for historians making digital projects. I found myself totally uninterested in the exploratory site. I suppose that if I were a food historian or a historian of urban culture I might get more out of it. But unless I am motivated to examine five thousand menus ... I'm not motivated to explore five thousand menus. Pardon the pun, but the exploratory version leaves the sources undigested.

By contrast, the narrative version was highly engaging. We get to see plenty of menus, but the menus we see were selected because they make a point. And the narrative version tells us what we can learn about class, gender, and environmental history from these sources. Here is the difference:

> The thousands of menus in this collection document a period of fundamental transformation---and the birth of the society and restaurant that we know today.
> 
> I've searched through the archive to tell you that story in ten dishes.

The narrative version is useful because the author put in the work to go from thousands of menus to ten dishes.[^1] The author didn't just find the sources and make them available to others: he figured out what the historical questions were and what the answers are. 

Often digital historians believe they are doing a service to their users by providing the underlying sources. But I don't think that is really the case, at least if they don't also provide a reasonably full narrative or argumentative account of what can be learned from the sources. Most digital history projects remain underdeveloped because they don't do the work of historical interpretation that gives the reader a way in to the history. 

[^1]: Stephen Lurie, "A History of Menus Is a Menu of History," _The Pudding_ (June 2026): <https://pudding.cool/2026/06/menu-story/>.
