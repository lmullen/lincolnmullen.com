A sentence that has stuck with me since I [read it](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2026/06/ai-open-ai-anthropic/687689/) this summer:

> A guiding principle of the emerging AI age is this: When intelligence is plentiful, volition is valuable.

Earlier I [wrote](/blog/behind-ahead/) that when it came to programming, "my rare and valuable skill was still valuable but no longer rare." I am still adjusting to that world. The post in _The Atlantic_ linked above is primarily about cultivating individual mental effort, which, to be sure, is something that I worry about. But I've also been thinking about that sentence in the context of making good history.

My ability to just make things has never been higher. Even now, I am itching to vibe up a little command line utility that will put a widget on my desktop showing me how many blog posts I've written each month. It would take fifteen minutes at most.

But a widget is not a project. A project requires one to, well, project: to exert one's will and attention in a sustained way. I am certainly finding that to be true with one of my digital projects, [Legal Modernism](https://legalmodernism.org). In retrospect it is clear to me that a year ago the complexity of that project exceeded my skill as a computational historian, at least as long as I was going to maintain my standards for accuracy and truthfulness. The advent of Claude Code raised my skill.

But the project has demanded even more from me in terms of attention and volition. I don't mean simply that I am still serving as the architect of the computational apparatus, though that has certainly grown more demanding. Rather, the project has required all the more attention to the basics of historical research: What is the right historical question? Does the evidence answer the question? Can I select and arrange the evidence to make a meaningful interpretation of the past?

I'm not sure I have the right words to capture the feeling, but trying to answer those questions skillfully feels very much like an act of sustained volition. To quote Frog and Toad, those two lovers of wisdom: "We need will power."
