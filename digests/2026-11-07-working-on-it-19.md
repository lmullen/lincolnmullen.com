## [Two kinds of computational historical thinking](https://lincolnmullen.com/blog/two-kinds-of-computational-historical-thinking/)

<p class="woi-date"><a href="https://lincolnmullen.com/blog/two-kinds-of-computational-historical-thinking/">4 November 2026</a></p>

The [first semester](https://files.lincolnmullen.com/syllabi/clio3.2014.pdf) that I taught a (rather incoherent) course on computational history, the course was titled "Programming in History/New Media." The then-current state of that wing of digital history was that graduate students should learn to code. (Witness [*The Programming Historian*](https://programminghistorian.org).) Even at the time, I think I was trying to articulate that programming was merely a means to an end and that what we were really after was a way to think about history through computation. A good thing too, because programming came hard to the graduate students, but now an LLM can easily program anything a first-year graduate student can think of. So we may as well teach them to think computationally.

## [Where I draw the line](https://lincolnmullen.com/blog/where-i-draw-the-line/)

<p class="woi-date"><a href="https://lincolnmullen.com/blog/where-i-draw-the-line/">25 October 2026</a></p>

I [use AI](https://lincolnmullen.com/blog/behind-ahead/) for my work, for which this blog is a hub. Using AI necessarily raises questions of authorship and attribution. Who did the writing, the author or his agent? Is this blog the work of a machine or of my mind? Of course the mind is never unmediated by technology. I hope my readers will not begrudge me the use of the codex and the [text editor](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674417076) if I call the thoughts posted here my own. But still, having AI write is fundamentally different from using those other assistive technologies for writing, even though I don't know how to articulate why it is different.

So for myself at least, and perhaps for my readers, I find it necessary to draw the line where I will and won't use AI in my work. For the purposes of this website, here is where I draw the line right now.

## [Shadows and Solid Things](https://lincolnmullen.com/blog/shadows-and-solid-things/)

<p class="woi-date"><a href="https://lincolnmullen.com/blog/shadows-and-solid-things/">20 August 2026</a></p>

[_Shadows and Solid Things: A History of Religion and Archaeology in the Modern World_](https://uncpress.org/9781469698175/shadows-and-solid-things/) by Kris Zimmitti will be published this November. Kris writes beautifully and has an important tale to tell about how the study of the past through archaeology collided with mythic understandings of Bible times. If you've ever looked at a map in the back of a study Bible, wondered how long it took Joshua to march around Jericho, or looked at a line drawing of an ancient building in Sunday School, then this is the history that explains where those came from. 

[![The cover of Shadows and Solid Things](https://lincolnmullen.com/blog/shadows-and-solid-things/9781469698175.jpg)](https://uncpress.org/9781469698175/shadows-and-solid-things/)

> Archaeology began in an era when many people read ancient religious texts literally, believing that what was described on the page corresponded to a material reality that existed beneath the soil. With a shovel in one hand and a sacred text in the other, early archaeologists sought confirmation in concrete evidence. In the process, they opened themselves to the disturbing possibility that they would find nothing—or, perhaps even more unsettling, discover evidence that would challenge their cherished beliefs.
> 
> Using accounts of excavations in Italy, Egypt, Iraq, and Palestine, Kristofer B. Zimmitti explores the tensions between science and religion, faith and reason. As archaeology grew more popular during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, excavations from Herculaneum to Babylon and discoveries such as the Moabite Stone transformed the modern understanding of the ancient past for Protestants, Catholics, Jews, Muslims, and Latter-day Saints. While some archaeological findings confirmed the truth of ancient texts, others threatened to overturn them. Rather than abandon or question their faith, many believers kept digging. When the dust of excavation had settled, today’s religious world had been forged.

There’s not been a lot of optimism about the web recently, but Paul Ford [points out](https://ftrain.com/aboard-the-web-is-more-than-enough) that web technologies designed for teams of software engineers can now be used by individuals:

> The high tax of building for app stores seems less worth paying when you can just test an idea in the world from a web server for $0 after a couple weeks. Perhaps Mozilla should take another swing at launching a mobile phone! (Forget I mentioned that.) In any case, what used to be a large industrial process is now a personal one, if you so desire. Web browsers enable incredibly rich app development—and that’s radically easier than it used to be.

But then, this shrewd observation:

> It’s a mixed blessing. I’ve been a writer for years, and software development feels more and more like writing to me every passing week. The awkward part of the comparison is, in general, people do not want to pay writers any money at all. As a writer, you get used to it. But for software people, being perpetually devalued is pretty novel.

Paul Ford and Rich Ziade at the _Aboard_ podcast have a [good reminder](https://aboard.com/podcast/product-is-more-than-prompts/) that good software requires design---and thinking about it as a product---and not just functionality. 

In my world of DH projects, too many projects are just CRUD apps around a database. While I am [optimistic](https://lincolnmullen.com/blog/behind-ahead/) about the possibilities of building with LLMs, we need (I need) more product thinking: not what is most obvious from the database or sources, but what do users actually want to do with our projects.
