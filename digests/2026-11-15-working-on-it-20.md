## [Interpretation over exploration](https://lincolnmullen.com/blog/interpretation-over-exploration/)

<p class="woi-date"><a href="https://lincolnmullen.com/blog/interpretation-over-exploration/">15 November 2026</a></p>

_The Pudding_ is one of my favorite sites on the internet, full of fascinating and whimsical data visualizations. Occasionally the site takes on historical topics, as they recently did with historical menus from the New York Public Library. The site offered two looks at the menus. The first is an [exploratory interface](https://pudding.cool/2026/06/menu-collection/) to find the menus in the NYPL collection by city or other details, and to look at them directly. The second is an [interpretative story](https://pudding.cool/2026/06/menu-story/) explaining what can be learned from the menus.

![A screenshot of the thumbnails of the two visualizations from The Pudding website](https://lincolnmullen.com/blog/interpretation-over-exploration/pudding-menu-visualizations.png)

The two types of approach---explanatory and exploratory---offer a helpful comparison for historians making digital projects. I found myself totally uninterested in the exploratory site. I suppose that if I were a food historian or a historian of urban culture I might get more out of it since I'd want to develop my own thoughts. But unless I am motivated to examine five thousand menus ... I'm not motivated to explore five thousand menus. Pardon the pun, but the exploratory version leaves the sources undigested.

By contrast, the interpretative version was highly engaging. Readers get to see plenty of menus, but the menus we get to see were selected by the author because they make a point. And the interpretative version tells us what we can learn about class, gender, and environmental history from these sources. 

Here is the difference in a nutshell:

> The thousands of menus in this collection document a period of fundamental transformation---and the birth of the society and restaurant that we know today.
> 
> I've searched through the archive to tell you that story in ten dishes.

The interpretative version is useful because the author put in the work to go from thousands of menus to ten dishes.<sup>1</sup> The author didn't just find the sources and make them available to others: he figured out what the historical questions were and what the answers are. 

Often digital historians believe they are doing a service to their users by providing the underlying sources. But I don't think that is really the case, at least if they don't also provide a reasonably full narrative or argumentative account of what can be learned from the sources. Most digital history projects remain underdeveloped because they don't do the work of historical interpretation that gives the reader a way to understand the history.

<sup>1</sup> Stephen Lurie, "A History of Menus Is a Menu of History," _The Pudding_ (June 2026): <https://pudding.cool/2026/06/menu-story/>.

A few months late on my part, but here are John MacFarlane's [reflections on twenty years of creating and maintaining Pandoc](https://pandoc.org/twenty-years-of-pandoc.html). 

The first post I wrote about Pandoc [was in 2012](https://lincolnmullen.com/blog/pandoc-converts-all-your-text-documents/), a scant fourteen years ago. (Hard to believe it, but it used to be useful to write out command line invocations for others to learn from.) It is safe to say that anything I've ever published, and practically everything I've thought, at least professionally, started life as a Markdown file. And it started as a Markdown file because I knew I could get it out into a webpage, a beautifully typeset PDF, or (god forbid) a Word document thanks to Pandoc.
